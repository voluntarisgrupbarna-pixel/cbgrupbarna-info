Why girls quit basketball in their teens

At 15 and 16, only 8% of girls meet the recommended minimum physical activity levels. Boys reach 23.2%. The comfortable reading is that girls quit. The uncomfortable one is that the system pushes them out.

CB Grup Barna · 2026-08-14

The figure, and the trap in how we read it

The 2024-25 Sports Habits Survey found that only 8% of girls aged 15 and 16 meet minimum physical activity levels, against 23.2% of boys. Almost three times fewer.

The usual way of explaining it puts the verb on them: "they drop out," "they lose interest," "school gets in the way." But look at what's behind any single specific departure, and the verb switches sides. It isn't that they leave: it's that a point comes when carrying on becomes expensive, uncomfortable, or flatly impossible. And that doesn't happen by chance — it happens because of how clubs are designed.

The five mechanisms that push players out

At the club we've identified them and given them names, because we've seen them at work in other organisations and, at times, at home too. None of them is bad faith: they're organisational decisions no one made with the intent of pushing anyone out.

  • 1. No next step. Many clubs have girls' teams at mini and pre-youth level but none at junior or senior. When a cadet player looks for somewhere to continue, there's no place for her at her own club. She isn't quitting: she has nowhere to go.
  • 2. The hostile schedule. Senior women's teams are usually pushed into the 9:30 to 11pm slots — the ones no one else wants. A 17-year-old can't train at ten at night on a school night. The one who quits hasn't chosen anything.
  • 3. The empty bench. If a ten-year-old girl has never seen a woman coaching, she receives a message no one has said out loud: coaching is a man's job. Absence speaks too.
  • 4. Forced flight. Players with real potential have to move to Cornellà, Mataró or Girona to find a level that doesn't exist in Barcelona. At 16, that means choosing between basketball and the life you have.
  • 5. The access paradox. Support programmes for coaches usually require prior certification to help you get certified. A circular design that excludes exactly the people it meant to include.

Why exactly during puberty

Because it's when everything happens at once. Between ages 13 and 16 the brain reorganises its reward system, opening a window of vulnerability where stable routines are worth their weight in gold. Organised sport works, at this stage, as external scaffolding: fixed days, people waiting for you, something you're good at.

It's also the age at which many families decide to pull sport activity "to focus on studies." Research points the other way: exercise improves concentration, memory and school performance. Pulling her out of the team in year 8 tends to cost you on both fronts.

And there's something rarely discussed: the clothing. Studies on adolescent dropout find, time and again, that constant self-consciousness about appearance caused by the uniform shows up among the reasons for quitting. We cover that separately in the article on the kit.

What a club should do

Nothing spectacular, and everything at once. Have a team in every women's category so no one ever has to leave. Distribute schedules by equity, not seniority. Have visible female coaches from day one. And don't run campaigns: just do it.

It's worth asking before signing anyone up. Is there a junior and senior women's team? What time does the senior women's team train? How many coaches are there? The answers say more than any statement of intent.

How we've set it up in El Clot

At CB Grup Barna there's a women's team at every category, from the 4-year-old Escoleta to senior level, so no player ever has to fend for herself when she moves up an age group. Women's teams get priority access to the central afternoon slots. And there are 38 active female coaches, 65.5% of the technical staff, coaching women's, mixed and men's teams alike.

The club puts 50% of its budget into this when the sector norm is around 35%. Not because it's an equality policy, but because that's how it's been built since 1965.

Frequently asked questions

At what age do most girls quit sport?

The critical point falls between ages 13 and 16. At 15-16, only 8% of girls meet the recommended minimum physical activity levels, against 23.2% of boys.

Is it true that quitting sport improves academic results?

Research points the other way: regular physical exercise improves concentration, memory and school performance. Pulling a teenager out of sport to study more tends to backfire.

What should I ask a club to know if it will keep my daughter playing?

Three questions say almost everything: whether there's a junior and senior women's team, what time the senior women's team trains, and how many female coaches the club has.

Why does it matter to have female coaches?

Because a girl who never sees a woman on the bench receives the implicit message that coaching isn't for her. Sustained exposure to female coaches reverses this bias, for girls, boys and families alike.

Does CB Grup Barna have a women's team at every category?

Yes. From the 4-to-7 Escoleta all the way to senior, through premini, mini, pre-youth, cadet and junior. In the 2026-27 season there are 8 women's teams in FCBQ federated competition.
