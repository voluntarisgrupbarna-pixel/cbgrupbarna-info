Executive summary

Six conclusions the whole document rests on

  1. Development-first isn't ideology: it's what the evidence says. Fun predicts sporting continuity (r = 0.45) almost three times better than competitive performance (r = 0.18), and 92.8% of who stands out at junior level isn't who stands out at senior level. A club that retains players by winning uses the weakest lever there is.
  2. The model has a famous, documented precedent. In 2014 Marc Gasol founded a school — not a team — and didn't create his first senior team until 2017. Thirteen verified public pieces, from 2014 to 2026, support his doctrine: "We start with maximum humility, with commitment, effort and our own methodology" and "We're not going to rush to reach the ACB."
  3. The Barna is the third-largest development structure in its comparable universe. 32 federated teams in the FCBQ 2026-27 (28 development + 4 senior). Only Boet Mataró (42) and UE Mataró (37) field more — and of the three, the Barna is the only one with no team in an FEB category.
  4. First development club in Barcelona city by digital community. 7,096 followers, 6th of 22 verified Catalan and Spanish development clubs. The five ahead are all from the metropolitan area, not the city itself.
  5. Where others need two or three entities, the Barna does it with one. Barça, Girona, Manresa, Sant Adrià, Cornellà and L'Hospitalet split men's, women's and grassroots across separate corporations and accounts. The Barna covers everything under a single brand: for sponsorship, a single point of entry to the whole community.
  6. The ceiling isn't the structure, it's the conversion. 222 followers per federated team, against 532 for Sant Adrià or 455 for Almeda. The structure is already built; what's missing is editorial identity.
How to read this report. Parts I to III answer why we develop players this way and where the model comes from. Parts IV to VI answer where we stand in comparable numbers. The final methodological note documents the discrepancies between sources and which one governs in each case.
Part I

The executive summary, in full

Complete reproduction of the Executive summary of the state of the question (Ana Fernández Durán, August 2026), the only research document attached in this session.

I·1 · What this document is

A doctoral-scope review

Summary of a doctoral-scope literature review on the grassroots basketball club: eleven parallel research lines, more than 900 queries against academic search engines, institutional repositories, bibliographic APIs and primary federation, legal and government sources. The full report runs to ~61,000 words and eighteen chapters.

It covers young-athlete psychology, drop-out, talent development, maturation and health, parents and coaches, child protection, club sociology, international models, management and governance, sport theory, gender and feminism, and the economics of basketball clubs. It includes a map of Catalan basketball and a chapter applied to CB Grup Barna.

Its distinctive trait is traceability. Every reference carries a verification mark, and Annex III exhaustively lists what could not be confirmed — including five widely-circulating sector figures that must not be cited.

I·2 · Findings

The fifteen findings that matter most

On drop-out

Finding 1
12.93

Drop-out clusters at ages 12–14, not 11

Real average age: 12.93. It matches exactly the infantil → cadet transition in Spanish grassroots basketball.

Finding 2
r = 0.45

Fun is the dominant predictor of persistence

Sporting competence is one of the weakest (r = 0.18). A club that retains players through performance uses the weakest lever there is.

Finding 3
86 vs 58%

What retains players isn't pressure but presence

Basketball players report the highest levels of negative parental behaviour of all the sports analysed. And what retains them: 86% parental attendance at games among current players, versus 58% among former players.

Finding 4
70 · 59%

Not loss of interest: environmental expulsion

70% of Catalan girls drop out on reaching adolescence, yet 59% of those who drop out still enjoy competitive sport.

On talent

Finding 5
92.8%

Early detection doesn't work

92.8% of junior and senior elite populations are different people.

Finding 6
50.3 / 6.8

Relative age selects but doesn't predict performance

In Spain it's extreme: 50.3% born in the first quarter and 6.8% in the fourth among regional selections (n = 1,368).

Finding 7
U12–U15

Maturation predicts physique, not skill

Biological maturation predicts power, speed and agility, but not technical skill. Selecting on physique at U12–U15 is, to a large extent, selecting by biological birth date.

On health and club culture

Finding 8
−36%

Ten minutes of neuromuscular warm-up

2–3 times a week, cuts youth basketball ankle and knee injuries by 36% — with equivalent effect with or without outside supervision.

Finding 9
Elite

The serious problems come from the best players

Hughes and Coakley's sport ethic explains harm through over-conformity: the player who hides pain isn't breaking the club's culture, they're embodying it. Burnout is a problem of the club's social organisation, not a personal failure by the player.

Finding 10
78.1%

Violence and positive experience coexist

78.1% of Spanish respondents to the European CASES project reported interpersonal violence within sport before age 18 (69.4% psychological) — and 85% simultaneously reported positive experiences. Both figures coexist.

On economics

Finding 11
×5 · ×36

The cost ladder per promotion is discontinuous

×5 at the first jump to national level, ×2–3 at each step after that. The first step — the one a neighbourhood club takes — is the harshest in relative terms. From Tercera to Primera FEB, the fee multiplies by 36 and the bond by nearly 19.

Finding 12
2 cases

The adjustment lands on the grassroots and the women's section

Força Lleida, 2024: the men's promotion to the ACB was funded, among other things, by withdrawing the women's team from Liga Femenina 2. Betis / Baloncesto Sevilla, 2023: a €2.6M cut eliminating two youth teams and the minibasket and pre-infantil categories — and still ended in insolvency in 2026 and the club's disappearance.

Finding 13
0.61%

The FEB spends 0.61% of its budget on development

€193,762 against more than €8 million on high-performance competition.

On gender and governance

Finding 14
90 → 40%

Equal access doesn't produce equal power

Title IX multiplied US high-school female athletes ninefold and cut female coaches from 90% to 40%. In Catalonia, male presidencies of sports clubs have stayed pinned at 88% for six years while women's licences keep growing.

Finding 15
~84%

The dominant constraint isn't money: it's the board

Three independent sources rank it above funding as the perceived problem, and ~84% of the economic value of Catalan sports volunteering falls on the boards of directors.

I·3 · The intervention

The best documented cost-to-impact ratio

A 75-minute-to-2-hour coach behavioural-training workshop cut drop-out from 26% to 5% in a controlled-group design
Barnett, Smoll and Smith, 1992

Three independent lines converge with it: the coach is the dimension that best predicts retention at Spanish sports centres; caring/transformational climate outperforms every other predictor of youth outcomes (r = 0.43, across 177 studies); and in the SPLISS model, the pillar that sets Serbia and Lithuania apart isn't funding but coach training.

Necessary caveat: a four-month Spanish study cut punitive behaviour by more than half without improving the climate perceived by players, and two systematic reviews note that institutional maintenance is almost never documented. Training once isn't enough: it has to be recurring, and it should measure the climate players perceive, not coach attendance.

I·5 · Talking points

What can be stated to the Board, the Districte and sponsors

Can be stated

  • TEAM sport behaves in the opposite direction to individual sport on adolescent mental health: −10% anxiety/depression, −19% depressive symptoms with withdrawal and −20% transgressive behaviour among girls, versus +16% anxiety/depression in individual sport (n = 11,235). It's the most specific argument a basketball club has available.
  • The social value of an active minor is almost double that of an active adult: £4,300/year (ages 11–16) versus £2,600.
  • A volunteer generates more than double the social value per head of a participant (£2,663 versus £1,274).
  • Sport's social return has a methodologically published measurement: an SROI of 1.91 (2019), 3.28 (2020) and 4.38 (2025) per pound invested.
  • The club network is inherited, non-reproducible capital. A club with sixty years of history in El Clot is not a replaceable service provider.
  • In Sant Martí, the income gap between neighbourhoods in the same district is nearly 3:1 (Vila Olímpica +41% above Barcelona's average; El Besòs i el Maresme −63%). The fee, the kit and travel aren't administrative details: they're the barrier.
  • Basketball is the Spanish team sport with the best gender trend: 36.1% women's licences in 2025, with women's growth in 2024 more than double that of men's.

Cannot be stated

  • That sports participation causes integration, better academic performance or better mental health. The study designs are mostly cross-sectional and the effects are small (10–20%).
  • That sport improves grades. The effect on academic performance is mixed; the solid effect is on sustained classroom attention (d = 0.77) and on self-esteem. It's a better argument and it's also true.

The defensible formulation is Coalter's: sport is a necessary but not a sufficient condition. What produces the effects are the sustained social relationships and the complementary support the club organises around the sport.

I·6 · Off-limits zone

Five figures that circulate and must NOT be cited

"70% of children drop out before age 13"
No verifiable primary source. Use instead: 12.93-year average drop-out age and 30% annual drop-out from at least one club.
"±5 years of maturational variability"
Unverifiable. Use instead: a PHV range of 12.8 to 16.5 years within a single, already-preselected academy.
"The 10,000-hour rule"
Journalistic popularisation. Deliberate practice explains 18% of the variance in sports and only 1%, not significant, among elite athletes.
Specific percentages of women's-sport TV coverage
Not verified in full text. What is verified: "little change over the past 30 years" and "dismally low."
"For every euro invested in sport, €2 of social return" (Spain)
A press statement from an institution, not a published finding. Use instead: the English ratios.
I·7 · Closing

The sentence that sums up the work

"Participation in sport is a necessary, but not sufficient condition to obtain the supposed benefits"
Fred Coalter, after eight years auditing the evidence on the social value of sport
"L'esport és una eina per educar"
Marc Gasol, explaining why his project started with a school and not a first team

A tool. Not a miracle. What it produces depends entirely on how it's used — and that, exactly, is the responsibility and the opportunity of a development club.

Part II

The thesis, chapter by chapter

A map of the 133 pages and ~61,500 words of the full report, reconstructed from its own table of contents and the working notes from the session in which it was produced. The full PDF isn't attached in this session: to reproduce any chapter word for word, simply attach it again.

II·1 · Structure

Eleven research lines, eighteen chapters, three annexes

  1. Young-athlete psychology Motivation, motivational climate, self-determination; why fun predicts persistence and performance doesn't.
  2. Sports drop-out The real age (12.93), the infantil→cadet transition, 30% annual drop-out from at least one club, and the specific case of female drop-out as environmental expulsion.
  3. Talent development Early detection (the 92.8%), the relative-age effect (50.3%/6.8%), early specialisation and its alternatives.
  4. Maturation and health PHV (range 12.8–16.5 years in a preselected academy), injuries and neuromuscular warm-up (−36%), training load.
  5. Parents and coaches Parental behaviour (basketball, the worst sport analysed), presence as retention (86% vs. 58%), and coach behavioural training (26%→5%).
  6. Child protection LOPIVI, the European CASES project (78.1% / 69.4% / 85%), protection officer, club culture.
  7. Club sociology The club as inherited, non-reproducible social capital; volunteering (~84% of value on boards); the board as the dominant constraint.
  8. International models The SPLISS model; why Serbia and Lithuania stand out for coach training rather than budget.
  9. Management and governance Professionalisation, theory of change, the annual report as a fundraising instrument.
  10. Sport theory Bourdieu (capital and field), Elias (the civilising process and sport), Hughes and Coakley's sport ethic (over-conformity), and Bronfenbrenner (the ecological model of development). The framework that explains why burnout is organisational, not individual.
  11. Feminism and gender From Ann Hall and Iris Marion Young to Messner and Theberge; gender-bland sexism; the obligations of Law 39/2022. The most usable takeaway: Young translated into on-court technical goals — "the ball is coming for me," the discontinuous unit in shooting, occupying space as a season-long objective.

Plus the cross-cutting chapters: the chapter 11 dedicated to Marc Gasol (Part III), the promotion dilemma (ch. 14), the map of Catalan basketball (ch. 15), the chapter applied to CB Grup Barna, the 12-chapter editorial series (§17.12) with the table of which evidence backs each piece, and the three annexes.

II·2 · Chapter 14

The promotion dilemma

Using official FEB and ACB figures: the discontinuous cost ladder (×5 at the first national jump; from Tercera to Primera FEB the fee is ×36 and the bond ×19) and the two documented cases that are exactly the club's thesis:

  • Força Lleida, 2024. The men's promotion to the ACB was funded, among other things, by withdrawing the women's team from Liga Femenina 2.
  • Betis / Baloncesto Sevilla, 2023. Cut €2.6M by eliminating two youth teams and the minibasket and pre-infantil categories — and still ended in insolvency in 2026, and the club disappeared.

The chapter's operational conclusion: the written promotion policy with a financial threshold and a no-cannibalisation clause (move #5) exists so this can't happen to the Barna.

II·3 · Chapter 15

The map of Catalan basketball

National-level clubs were verified in full; regional ones only partially, because the FCBQ's website blocked automated access with a captcha. The chapter's most striking finding: there isn't a single Catalan club in Primera FEB.

This line was later completed with the "Piràmide CB Grup Barna" report (13/08/2026, records from basquetcatala.cat): 33 federated Barna teams against the 23 development teams of the Fundació Bàsquet Girona, the 40 of Boet Mataró (with no women's senior B), and the 24 of Santfeliuenc — with the Barna the only one of the four with two senior teams (A+B) in both genders within the same entity.

III·2 · Synthesis

The Gasol model in five ideas, all sourced

  • School first, team after. He founds the school in 2014; the first senior team doesn't arrive until 2017 (EBA). Three years of grassroots before a single big team. (FEB 2014 and 2017; Esport3)
  • Three principles, not a slogan: effort, commitment and methodology — a methodology that's "his own," shared across every category. (FEB 2014, 2017; the club's talk at the UdG with Jesús Escosa and Marc Pena)
  • No rush, even while winning: "We're not going to rush to reach the ACB" — said weeks before promotion. Solidity sets the pace, not the result. (HoyMagazine 2022)
  • The person before the sport: "Basketball isn't as important as the message we can send to younger generations to inspire them to be good people." (El Desmarque, 2019)
  • Sport as an educational tool: "L'esport és una eina per educar" — the formulation the Barna's research chose to sum up the whole body of work. (CBGB thesis, 2026)

It's, point by point, the same order CB Grup Barna champions in its sporting project — with the measurable difference that the Barna has been doing it for sixty years and keeps grassroots and senior teams within the same entity.

Part VI

Diagnosis, targets and talking points

What the data proves, what it doesn't prove, and what can be said with it in front of a Board, the Districte or a sponsor without exaggerating a single figure.

VI·1 · Diagnosis

Four honest readings

What the data proves

That the club communicates above its sporting category and in line with its real structural size. That the brand — neighbourhood, development, women's, values — generates adherence without needing an FEB senior team to tow an audience along. And that the argument is twofold: community and structure, both verifiable in public sources.

What the data does NOT prove

Not reach, not engagement, not a commercial value equivalent to a professional club. UEM and Prat have federation TV and FEB box office; Sant Adrià has a women's-only story with a purity a mixed account can't replicate. Community size is one variable, not the full picture.

The internal anomaly

32 federated teams and around 450 families sustain 7,096 followers: 222 per team. Specialists convert at double that rate (Sant Adrià, 532; Almeda, 455). The ceiling isn't set by the market or the structure — it's the third largest in the compared universe — it's set by the conversion of identity into audience.

The untapped advantage

Those above are there via one of two routes: an FEB senior team (UEM, Prat, Cornellà) or niche purity (Sant Adrià, Almeda). The Barna has a third route no one in the group occupies: being the media outlet of grassroots basketball — tactics, health, families, coaches — from the single account of a club that does it all.
