Why girls quit basketball in their teens

At 15 and 16, only 8% of girls meet the recommended minimum physical activity levels. Boys reach 23.2%. The comfortable reading is that they quit. The uncomfortable one is that the system is pushing them out.

CB Grup Barna · 2026-08-14

The figure, and the trap in how we read it

The 2024-25 Sports Habits Survey says only 8% of girls aged 15 and 16 meet the minimum physical activity levels, versus 23.2% of boys. Almost three times fewer.

The usual way of explaining it puts the verb on them: "they quit," "they lose interest," "school gets in the way." But when you look at what's behind each specific departure, the verb switches sides. It isn't that they leave: it's that a moment arrives when continuing becomes expensive, uncomfortable or flatly impossible. And that doesn't happen by chance — it happens because of how clubs are designed.

The five mechanisms that push players out

At the club we've identified them and they have names, because we've seen them at work at other organisations and, at some point, at home too. None of them is malice: they're organisational decisions nobody made while thinking about pushing anyone out.

  • 1. There's no next rung. Many clubs have women's teams at mini and youth level, but not at junior or senior. When a cadet player looks for where to continue, there's no room at her club. She doesn't quit: she has nowhere to go.
  • 2. The hostile schedule. Women's senior teams are usually relegated to the 9:30 to 11:00 pm slots, the ones no one else wants. A 17-year-old can't train at ten at night on a school night. The one who quits hasn't chosen anything.
  • 3. The empty bench. If a ten-year-old girl has never seen a woman coaching, she receives a message no one has said out loud: leading is a man's job. Absence speaks too.
  • 4. The forced exodus. Players with potential have to move to Cornellà, Mataró or Girona to find the level that doesn't exist in Barcelona. At 16, that means choosing between basketball and the life you have.
  • 5. The access paradox. Support programmes for coaches usually require a prior qualification to help you get the qualification. A circular design that excludes exactly who it meant to include.

Why puberty specifically

Because that's when everything happens at once. Between ages 13 and 16 the brain reorganises its reward system, opening a window of vulnerability in which stable routines are worth gold. Organised sport functions, at this stage, as external scaffolding: fixed days, people waiting for you, something you're good at.

It's also the age when many families decide to pull out of sport "to focus on studies." Research points the other way: exercise improves concentration, memory and school performance. Taking her off the team in the second year of secondary school tends to cost on both fronts.

And there's something rarely discussed: clothing. Studies on teenage dropout find, time and again, that constant worry about physical appearance because of the uniform shows up among the reasons for quitting. We cover that separately in the article on kit design.

What a club should do

Nothing spectacular, and everything at once. Have a team at every women's category so no one ever has to leave. Distribute schedules fairly, not by seniority. Have visible women coaches from day one. And don't run campaigns about it: just do it.

It's worth asking before signing anyone up anywhere. Is there a women's junior and senior team? What time does the women's senior team train? How many coaches are there? The answers say more than any statement of intent.

How we've set it up in El Clot

At CB Grup Barna there's a women's team at every category, from the Escoleta at age 4 through to senior, so no player has to fend for herself when she moves up an age group. Women's teams get priority access to the central evening slots. And there are 38 active coaches, 65.5% of the technical staff, who coach women's, mixed and men's teams.

The club puts 50% of its budget into that when the sector norm is around 35%. Not because it's an equality policy, but because that's how it's been built since 1965.

Frequently asked questions

At what age do most girls quit sport?

The critical point falls between ages 13 and 16. At 15-16, only 8% of girls meet the recommended minimum physical activity levels, versus 23.2% of boys.

Is it true that quitting sport improves academic results?

Research points the other way: regular physical exercise improves concentration, memory and school performance. Pulling a teenager out of sport to study more tends to backfire.

What should I ask a club to know if it will keep my daughter playing?

Three questions say almost everything: whether there's a women's team at junior and senior level, what time the women's senior team trains, and how many women coaches the club has.

Why does it matter to have women coaches?

Because a girl who never sees a woman on the bench receives the implicit message that leading isn't for her. Sustained exposure to women coaches reverses this bias, for girls, boys and families alike.

Does CB Grup Barna have a women's team at every level?

Yes. From the Escoleta at ages 4 to 7 through to senior, including premini, mini, youth, cadet and junior. In the 2026-27 season there are 8 women's teams competing in the FCBQ federated league.
